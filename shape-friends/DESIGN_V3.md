# Rethink: a toy world, not another skin

ARCHIVED DESIGN STUDY: the user rejected the picture-book direction and subsequently approved the detailed ink-and-painted garden adventure. See README.md and ASSETS.md for the implemented direction. The original Pip’s Garden remains outside this redesign. The broader 30-minute adventure is still a design target, not a verified result. The remainder of this document records earlier proposals, not current feature claims.

## What the current build is missing

- Cards are placed over a background; they do not feel like objects in a world.
- Sparky is expressive in still images but has little physical relationship with play.
- Successful matching changes a counter and collects thumbnails. The reward is not memorable enough.
- Backdrop, glossy source objects, synthetic UI geometry and mascot have inconsistent material treatment.
- Progress badges, repeated card-back faces and large callouts compete with the pictures the child should notice.
- The picnic still presents discoveries mostly as an inventory grid. Bouncing an icon is not a rich toy interaction.
- Device speech is functional, not a finished character performance. A custom voice remains a separate production requirement.

## Proposed direction

A small handcrafted garden playhouse seen from a slightly elevated viewpoint. Warm maple wood, pale mint linen, dusty coral, cream paper, sage plants and peach blossoms. Rounded, tactile picture tiles on a real play surface. Sparky leans into the activity and looks toward the child or chosen tile. No large screen title, progress bar or permanent instruction panel.

The supplied Sparky identity remains recognizable. This is an original toy-world direction, not a copy of a named franchise or artist. Art consistency matters more than adding detail or increasing saturation.

The Grade 1 objective and exact four GDD rounds stay unchanged. So do fair observed-card memory, strict alternating turns, eight-card boards, persistent discoveries and accessible native input.

## Play experience

1. Sparky places the final tile and gives a brief inviting gesture. The child can immediately choose a tile.
2. A tile lifts before turning; the object appears at generous scale. The second choice gets a short held beat so the child can compare shapes.
3. On a match, a simple shared shape contour connects the two illustrations. The objects then leave a small trail toward the discovery world. Matching means **the same shape**, not simply a success tick.
4. Each shape family has a concrete payoff: round things roll down a little ramp; box-like things open a toy compartment; cones become party props; cylinders make a tiny water/music interaction. These are short, optional play opportunities, not extra tests or mandatory minigames.
5. Sparky participates visibly, with anticipation, pointing, reaction and a quiet return to rest. He never plays a second consecutive turn or uses an unseen card.
6. New objects join a shared toy scene in the foreground. They remain touchable and persistent. Completion clears the matching surface and expands the same world instead of navigating to a gallery page.

## Quality gates before calling the redesign finished

- A still screenshot reads as a cohesive game scene without explanation.
- The visual target is reviewed before another wholesale asset/UI implementation.
- All eight tiles are easy to hit and read at 320 px width; controls never overlap them.
- Sparky's size, baseline, shadows and style remain consistent through gestures.
- Matching has a legible educational connection and an enjoyable physical payoff.
- The first 20 seconds include a meaningful interaction beyond a generic card flip.
- The final discovery space is a toy to play with, not an inventory screen.
- Motion, touch cancellation, pause, mute, storage failures and keyboard alternatives remain safe.
- No claim of a finished custom character voice while device speech is still the fallback.
